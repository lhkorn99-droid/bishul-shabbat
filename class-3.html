Kli rishon, kli sheni, kli shlishi

Study aid for the third shiur: which vessel can cook and why, the stringency that treats every food as easily cooked, what counts as a liquid or a spice, how to make tea and coffee, melting ice, and the chumra of the ambati.

Source: שולחן ערוך 318 Full recording

The three vessels

18:52

The syllabus said bishul achar bishul tonight; the Rabbi switched the order so he can use these terms when that topic comes. Class 1 set when something counts as cooking (reaching yad soledes bo). This class asks which vessels have the strength to cook at all.

1 · כלי ראשון

Kli rishon Cooks

The vessel the food was heated in: the urn, the pot of soup, the cholent pot. It stays a kli rishon after it comes off the fire, so a raw carrot may not go into a hot pot of soup on the counter either. 22:14

Off the fire, is it de'oraisa? The Yerushalmi implies only derabanan: a gezeira because the pot used to be on the fire. The Bavli implies it truly cooks, and the poskim rule that way: a kli rishon off the fire cooks mide'oraisa.

2 · כלי שני

Kli sheni Does not cook

Anything filled from a kli rishon: the cup from the urn, the serving dish from the pot, the bowl of soup. The Mishnah says it does not cook, even while it is far above yad soledes bo. 24:07

3 · כלי שלישי

Kli shlishi Even more lenient

Filled from a kli sheni: a second cup, or the bowl ladled from the serving dish. Whether it adds anything beyond a kli sheni is a machlokes, below. 1:03:29

Exceptions in both directions

Kalei habishul and basar shor

Stricter: some delicate foods cook so easily that they may not go even into a kli sheni. The Mishnah lists salted fish and kolias ha'ispanin (a kind of fish). 26:06

More lenient: the Gemara says ox meat is so tough that only fire itself cooks it, so it may go into a kli rishon off the fire. Not practical, though: the moisture in the meat still cooks. It only shows that the kli rishon rule is about ordinary food. 27:00

Why can't a kli sheni cook?

25:30

The obvious answer, that it is cooler, does not work. The Rabbi showed a kli sheni that is hotter than a kli rishon.

  • If it were only a gezeira (the Yerushalmi's view), the answer is easy: the pot was once on the fire, so the Rabbis worried you would add food while it was there; a cup was never on the fire. But we rule a kli rishon off the fire cooks de'oraisa, so that answer is not available.
  • Tosafos: the walls. A kli rishon's walls are hot, so it holds its heat. A kli sheni's walls are cold, so it keeps cooling, holech u'miskarer. 39:00
  • The problem: you may not dip a carrot in the pot even for ten seconds, and in the next ten seconds the cup is still the hotter one. How do cold walls help now? 41:16
The answer · 44:05

The question is the vessel's strength, not the next ten seconds

Nothing cooks in ten seconds, even in a pot on the fire. The ten-second dip is forbidden for the reason from class 1: you may not put food anywhere it could cook if left there, in case you forget it. A kli rishon holds its heat long enough to bring food to the start of cooking, so even a quick dip is forbidden. A kli sheni cools too fast to ever get there, so leaving food in it is harmless, even while it is boiling hot right now. The same idea explains kalei habishul: those foods do cook in the weaker heat, so they stay out of a kli sheni.

Liran's suggestion during class, that you might leave it in the pot longer, was "on the right track." The Rabbi credited this explanation to a Rishon whose work was printed later; the name is unclear in the recording. Asked whether thick walls change things: in principle maybe, but halacha does not make that distinction.

The stringency: every food may be kalei habishul

56:15

The Gemara permits almost any raw food in a kli sheni. In practice we put almost nothing in. Here is why.

The Mishnah's list is not complete. The Sefer Yereim (R' Eliezer of Metz, a talmid of Rabbeinu Tam) proves it from salt. The Gemara records a machlokes whether salt is like ox meat (cooks only on the fire) or kalei habishul (not even in a kli sheni). If the Mishnah's list were complete, salt's absence would settle that machlokes. The Gemara does not treat it that way, so the list is only examples.

So we cannot know which foods are kalei habishul, and we treat every food as if it might be. Contemporary poskim name raw eggs as truly kalei habishul, and some mention instant oats.

What we know may go into a kli sheni

  • Liquids. The Gemara shows water (cooling a bath) and oil (warming a flask of oil in hot water, like warming a baby bottle). The Alter Rebbe reads this as any natural liquid. The Mishnah Berurah allows only water and oil, though for milk he will agree for another reason (it is already pasteurized).
  • Spices (tavlin). The Gemara says you may not pour hot water onto spices, but you may put them into a kli sheni.
  • Everything else: not into a kli sheni.

Does a kli shlishi help?

1:03:29

Strict view

The Yereim: no difference. In the source the Shulchan Aruch quotes, he says explicitly that bread or matzah may go neither into a sheni nor a shlishi. It also makes sense: if the worry is that a food is easily cooked, a third cup has the same cold walls and hot water as the second. Many families follow this and do not use a kli shlishi for anything they would not put in a kli sheni.

Common practice

The Mishnah Berurah, citing the Pri Megadim: in kashrus we distinguish. A fleishig spoon in a bowl of hot soup (kli sheni) must be kashered; soup that spilled onto a milchig counter (kli shlishi) does not make it treif. Since keeping food out of a kli sheni is itself only a chumra, we need not extend it further. This is the accepted practice. 1:13:09
The song from class: "There ain't no bishul in a kli shlishi." One seminary gave the Rabbi a mug printed with his class drawings.
Salt · 1:15:33

Where salt lands

Raw, unprocessed salt: the letter of the law follows the lenient view, so it could even go into a kli rishon off the fire. But it is good to be strict for the other view and keep it out of a kli sheni. So, like other foods, salt goes into a kli shlishi. (Processed salt has another layer, coming with bishul achar bishul.)

What counts as a spice?

1:21:00

There is no single definition. Common ones: something added only to give flavor that you do not end up eating, or something you could reuse to flavor another dish.

  • Spices Chazal never knew. R' Chaim Naeh raises lemon, which they did not have: maybe Chazal meant only their own spices. The accepted approach: anything that meets the criteria of a spice counts.
  • Ground spices. The Shemiras Shabbos K'hilchasa is strict: Chazal meant a whole cinnamon stick or bay leaf; finely ground pepper or garlic powder cooks easily. Most poskim disagree: Chazal made a rule without regard to size. In practice it matters little, because the habit is to use a kli shlishi anyway.
  • Pepper, herbs, garnish (basil, cilantro), sugar: spices. Lemon probably is too; you may put it in but not squeeze it (a later topic).
  • Salt: fits the criteria, but Chazal singled it out as an exception (above).

Tea 1:29:15

Tea fits most definitions of a spice: it flavors the water and you do not even drink the leaves. Still:

  • Mishnah Berurah: worried tea is true kalei habishul, so not even in a kli shlishi. Those who follow him make tea essence before Shabbos and add it to a cup of hot water.
  • R' Moshe Feinstein: most probably a spice and could go in a kli sheni, but use a kli shlishi to be safe. The prevalent American practice.
  • Alter Rebbe: no direct proof. Rabbi Farkash understands the Tzemach Tzedek's criteria to include tea as a spice, so many Lubavitchers follow R' Moshe and use a kli shlishi.

But the water changes color! That does not prove cooking. A tea bag in cold water also colors and flavors it, just slower. 1:33:13

Bag first, or water first?

Putting the tea bag in an empty cup and pouring the kli sheni water onto it (iruy from a kli sheni) is fine. Pouring from a kli sheni has the status of a kli shlishi by most opinions, and the stream does not need to be broken. Some prefer adding the bag afterwards; there is even an argument from tata'ah gavar that pouring onto it is gentler. Bottom line: unless the tea is true kalei habishul, either order works.

Q: Is the tea bag itself a kli? No. Rishon, sheni and shlishi always describe the hot vessel. 1:39:12

Pouring onto instant coffee or sugar 2:01:10

Some worry the stream actively dissolves the granules, like crushing ice by hand, even with no bishul problem. Rabbi Farkash (building on R' Chaim Naeh) shows from the Alter Rebbe's Siddur that this is no problem: he forbids pouring from the urn onto sugar only because, since the sugar dissolves, it counts as cooking. If dissolving were forbidden in itself, pouring from any vessel would be a problem and his wording would make no sense. The heat dissolves it, not the force of the stream: pour cold water on sugar and nothing happens. 2:04:20

So: fill a cup from the urn, put the coffee in a second cup, pour onto it. "That's how I make my coffee."

Side topic: melting ice

1:45:38

Not bishul. Crushing ice by hand is forbidden derabanan because it looks like creating a new liquid; it fits none of the 39 melachos. (Some explain the problem with creating an electric current the same way; others disagree.)

Forbidden

Crushing ice by hand.

Only in need

Putting ice in a warm place to melt it, like the sun or near a radiator. A machlokes; the Alter Rebbe rules strictly unless there is a need: a baby's formula, drinks for guests. Never somewhere it could reach yad soledes bo, which is bishul. 1:48:37

Permitted

Ice in hot soup or iced coffee outside. It melts straight into liquid, so you never see a liquid being made. Ice is a liquid, so no bishul in a kli sheni. 1:51:43

Only in need

Making ice. Generally treated as the same machlokes in reverse. Some say freezing is fine by all; the Rabbi had not looked into it. Ice out of the freezer onto the table is fine by everyone. 1:52:42

Defrosting challah by the fire has no melting issue: no liquid is created. Automatic ice makers will come up with refrigerators and Shabbos mode.

The chumra of the ambati

2:06:33

A case in the Shulchan Aruch: a bath or mikveh filled with water heated elsewhere and piped in. It is a kli sheni, and liquids may go in a kli sheni, yet you may not add cold water to cool it.

Narrow reading

A rule for baths only. Baths are made extremely hot, and some add that the hot water keeps flowing in, almost connected to the kli rishon. A cup from the urn keeps the normal kli sheni rules: hot water with milk, or lemon and honey, with no second cup. The Beis Yosef holds a kli sheni never cooks, even if it is boiling.

The Chayei Adam

R' Avraham Danzig of Vilna, author of the Chayei Adam and Chochmas Adam, citing a Rambam: any kli sheni that is yad nichveis bo, hot enough to burn the hand, is treated as a kli rishon. A bowl of soup at 170°F is not; a cup straight from a 205°F urn is. Then even liquids and spices may not go in. This never applies to a kli shlishi. 2:08:24
Halacha lemaaseh · 2:13:03

Always make the third cup

Fill a cup from the urn, pour it into a second cup, then add the milk, sugar, honey, lemon, tea or essence there. Even if all you are adding is milk. "What are you losing out? Not even a cup," since you will use the first cup for something else afterwards.
