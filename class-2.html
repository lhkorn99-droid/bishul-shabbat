Kiruv Bishul and the four heats

Study aid for the second shiur: the urn question settled, the full chart of stirring, ladling and covering, the four kinds of heat, what happens to food that was cooked on Shabbos, and the practical rules for a pot that is not yet done.

Source: שולחן ערוך 318 Full recording

Recap: two numbers, two jobs

15:44

The window from class 1, restated. Solids run from maachal ben Drusai (itself a dispute among the Rishonim: a third cooked or half cooked) to mevushal kol tzorko. Liquids start at yad soledes bo. Where they end is the dispute: the Avnei Nezer says at a boil; the Alter Rebbe, as R' Moshe Feinstein reads him, says at yad soledes bo itself, and the shiur follows that. But yad soledes bo is not one number, so the Rabbi gave a rule for which figure to use when.

  • Use 110°F when the question is "might I be cooking?" The lower figure keeps you safe: anything warmer than that is treated as yad soledes bo.
  • Use 160°F when the question is "is this already cooked?" To rely on a liquid being past the window, it must have reached the higher figure.
  • Each number is the strict answer for its own question. The same spread of opinions is used in both directions, never the lenient way twice. (R' Moshe's 160°F comes from pasteurized wine: at that temperature evaporation is visible, so it is certainly yad soledes bo.)
  • A mixed pot has both sets of rules. Cholent is liquid and solids together, so you may not bring the liquid to yad soledes bo, and even once it boils you may not advance the cooking until every solid in it, meat, potatoes and beans, is fully cooked. "Fully cooked" always means everything in the pot.

The urn, settled

18:45

Class 1 left the external gauge as a dispute. This class gave the ruling and added a second question about the spout.

Permitted

The water in the tube. It sits outside the tank and gets to about 170°F without ever boiling. On the strict view that is uncooked water, and the first cup you draw on Shabbos pulls it into the tank, like pouring in water from the sink. On the lenient view it passed 160°F, so it counts as cooked and even the first cup is fine. The shiur follows the lenient view.
Stringency: draw a cup or two before Shabbos. The tube refills with water that actually boiled, and boiled water that has cooled only partly is still cooked by every opinion, so there is nothing left to worry about. (The Jewish-made urns put the tube inside the body for this reason.)

Permitted

The spout. The Mishnah Berurah's worry, originally about an earthenware pot over a flame: if it runs dry it cracks, so people rush to refill it, and on Shabbos that is cooking water. An electric urn run dry breaks too. Pump-top urns can only draw while enough water remains. Standard spouts are mounted high enough that a safe amount always stays behind, as long as you do not tilt the urn, so most poskim accept them as well.
Side point: sliding the urn to the counter edge so a tall cup fits under the spout is fine; it is not muktzeh, as long as you do not unplug it.

One more lid rule · 46:00

Condensation on the lid

The drops on a lid came from the pot, so they are cooked water. While they are still warm there is no problem putting the lid back (ein bishul achar bishul, next class). Once they have cooled completely they are subject to bishul again, and the lid must be dried first. The Rabbi's habit: set the lid down upside down. The hot lid, uncovered, lets the moisture evaporate, so it goes back dry. Face down, it traps the steam and you have to check.

Four kinds of heat

49:16

Bishul is about fire. Heat that comes from fire, directly or at one remove, is the melacha. Heat that comes from the sun is not, but the Rabbis fenced the look-alike case.

De'oraisa

Bishul b'or. Cooking on fire, or on electric heat, which halacha treats the same way.
בישול באור

De'oraisa

Toldos ha'or. Cooking on something the fire heated: a hot blech, a hot pan, the water in a kli rishon.
תולדות האור

Permitted

Bishul b'chama. Cooking in direct sunlight, like an egg set out on a hot day. Not the way cooking is done, so not the melacha.
בישול בחמה

Derabanan

Toldos chama. Cooking on something the sun heated, like a sun-baked rock. Permitted from the Torah, but a gezeira forbids it because it looks like toldos ha'or.
תולדות חמה

Why is the sun different? 1:05:36

Either way you are heating water to yad soledes bo, so why does the heat source matter? Two approaches. Rashi's: cooking in the sun is not the normal way to cook, a shinui so large that it is not just derabanan but permitted outright (like grinding with the back of a knife). The other: the melacha is defined by its heat source. Cooking means fire or a relative of fire; anything else is simply not bishul.

Where they differ: the microwave. It has no heat source at all, so on the second approach food cooked in it is not halachically cooked. On Rashi's approach, microwaving a potato is now a normal way to cook, so it is bishul. You cannot run a microwave on Shabbos anyway, but the question has a stringent side: if a microwaved potato was never "cooked," you may not later warm it in the cholent as if it were.

Two more cases

  • Hot springs (chamei Teveria): derabanan. The water is hot, but not from fire.
  • The car hood on a hot day: cracking an egg on it is toldos chama, since the metal, not the sun itself, does the cooking. Forbidden derabanan.
  • The hot water tap: drawing hot water lets cold water into the tank, where it is cooked by toldos ha'or. That is grama (indirect), but still forbidden. A solar tank in Israel is the same action at the derabanan level, which leaves a little more room in extenuating circumstances.
  • Electric is fire. Rav Chaim Ozer of Vilna made a berachah of borei me'orei ha'eish on an incandescent bulb to drive the point home. (LEDs are a separate discussion.)

Food that was cooked on Shabbos

1:19:07

The b'diavad half of the sugya. The answer depends on whether the cooking was deliberate, and on how cooked the food already was.

Shogeg: the machlokes

A Tannaic dispute. R' Meir permits food cooked by mistake even on Shabbos; R' Yehuda forbids it until after Shabbos. The Shulchan Aruch and the Alter Rebbe rule like R' Yehuda. No b'chdei sheya'asu wait is needed afterwards: that safeguard exists for things people take lightly, like asking a non-Jew or leaving a pot on an uncovered flame before Shabbos, so no one gains from doing them. Nobody cooks on Shabbos itself just to eat sooner after Shabbos.

The Vilna Gaon rules like R' Meir, based on Tosafos: Rav taught the strict rule in public but ruled leniently for a student, because the stringency was for simple people who might learn the wrong lesson. The Mishnah Berurah allows relying on the Gra in a case of great need or loss. The Alter Rebbe does not cite the Gra at all, so for those who follow him there is nothing to rely on, even in need.

How this carries over to other melachos (borer, carrying) is a separate discussion; there is more room to be lenient where the melacha did not change the item itself.

Already past ben Drusai? 1:42:04

The real-life case: Friday night someone lifts the cholent lid, sees the beans are not done, and puts the lid back. That is bishul, done b'shogeg. Can the cholent be eaten?

Mishnah Berurah (318 s.k. 27): a general rule. If there is a shitah that permits the act, then b'diavad we rely on it, even though we rule it is a Torah prohibition, and with no need for great loss. The Rambam and Rashba hold that maachal ben Drusai is the end of the window, so if the cholent was already past that point, it may be eaten. Less than that, only the Gra's leniency in a case of need.

R' Chaim Naeh: the Alter Rebbe states this rule only for a derabanan violation (for example, a pot that needed a blech and had none). For a de'oraisa, like cooking past ben Drusai, you cannot rely on a dissenting shitah. By derabanan everyone agrees the rule applies; by de'oraisa it is a machlokes between the Mishnah Berurah and R' Chaim Naeh. In practice, a shailah for a rav.

Halacha lemaaseh: before the cholent is done, do not disturb

1:55:00

The Rabbi's summary of the whole chart in one line: until everything in the pot is fully cooked, don't smell it, don't touch the cover, don't move it, don't go near it. Once it is fully cooked you can do almost anything except stir, and you ladle only off the fire.

High flame to low flame

Not fully cooked and it will burn overnight? You cannot lift it over to the lower flame. Lifting is a move to no flame, and from no flame back onto a flame is cooking. On a blech, slide it away from the fire instead; that only ever makes it cooler. Sliding toward a second, lower burner may pass through a cooler zone first, so that too is a problem.

Not sure it's all done?

Then treat it as not done. You may open it to look, but you may not put the cover back unless you are certain every ingredient is fully cooked. Hard, inedible bones don't count. Bones people chew on do have to be soft; if the only doubt is bones you don't plan to chew, there is room to be lenient (Rabbi Farkash).

The Friday-night mistake

Lifting the lid to check the cholent and putting it straight back while the beans are still hard is cooking b'shogeg. It happens in normal homes. What becomes of the cholent is the ben Drusai question above: by the Mishnah Berurah, fine if it was past halfway; by R' Chaim Naeh's reading of the Alter Rebbe, a shailah for a rav.
